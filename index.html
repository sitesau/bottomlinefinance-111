Construction Loans Comparison Explained

Major banks typically enforce conservative policies regarding presale requirements and leverage caps. They often demand a high percentage of pre-commitments before the first draw is released and adhere to strict lending templates. In contrast, non-bank lenders are often willing to consider reduced or nil presale cover. They assess the deal on its individual merits, looking at the strength of the feasibility and the clarity of the exit strategy. While banks might offer keener pricing for standard, low-risk deals, non-bank lenders provide crucial options for projects with tighter timelines or non-standard structures. Pricing reflects the higher risk tolerance, meaning the decision comes down to whether the return on equity justifies the cost of capital.

Progress Draw Structure and Interest

Unlike standard mortgages where funds are released upfront, these facilities operate on a progress draw system. The lender funds the build in stages, releasing capital only when specific milestones are reached. These stages are usually confirmed by a quantity surveyor to ensure work is completed to the required standard. A retention amount is often held back until practical completion. Interest efficiency is a key advantage here, as interest accrues only on the drawn balance rather than the total facility limit. Furthermore, interest is commonly capitalised within the facility across the construction period and settled at completion, assisting with cash flow during the build phase.

Gearing and Feasibility Metrics

Gearing is a critical metric when comparing facilities. Unlike a simple residential loan-to-value ratio, commercial construction loans are measured against two distinct figures. The loan to total development cost (LTC) measures the debt against the build expense, while the loan to value against gross realisable value (GRV) measures it against the end market value. A facility that tracks both offers a clearer picture of feasibility than a single snapshot ratio. Developers seeking to minimise their equity contribution often look for higher LTC and GRV limits. However, higher leverage usually correlates with higher interest rates. Understanding the interplay between these metrics helps developers select a facility that balances equity preservation with the cost of debt.

Presale and Exit Strategies

A comparison of lending options must scrutinise the presale conditions against the project's marketing velocity. Major banks generally require a significant portion of the project to be pre-sold to local buyers to mitigate risk. Non-bank lenders may be more flexible, prioritising a clear and realistic takeout strategy. This exit could involve a refinance to residual stock finance once practical completion is reached, or a bulk sale to an investor. The clarity of the exit is as important as the raw numbers. Lenders need to understand how the facility gets repaid and whether the timeline is realistic given the build program.

Asset Classes and Use Cases

The suitability of a facility often depends on the asset type being developed. Residential developments, ranging from townhouse projects and land subdivisions to apartment towers, are common candidates. Commercial developers funding retail, office, hotel or short-stay projects also rely on these structures. Industrial assets are particularly sought after due to strong tenant demand in many markets. Specialised assets like childcare centres require lenders who understand the specific operational model and long-term value. Non-bank lenders are frequently more willing to fund these specialised or rural agribusiness deals where the security and income stack up, even if they fall outside standard bank boxes.

Common questions

What is the difference between construction finance and development finance? Construction finance is the senior facility that funds the build itself. Development finance is a broader strategy that may also layer in mezzanine debt or preferred equity to lift total leverage across both land and build.

Why choose a non-bank lender for a project? Non-bank lenders are often chosen when a project requires higher leverage, has lower presale cover, or has a non-standard structure that does not fit the rigid policy boxes of major banks.

How are funds released during the build? Funds are released through progress draws as specific build stages are reached. These stages are usually verified by a quantity surveyor before the capital is advanced.
