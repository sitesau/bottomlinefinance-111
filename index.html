Construction Loans Comparison Explained

Lenders determine the cost and size of a facility based on several critical variables. The primary metrics are the loan to total development cost (LTC) and the loan to value against gross realisable value (GRV). A facility that tracks both metrics provides a clearer picture of project feasibility than a single loan-to-value snapshot. Strong presale cover and lower gearing ratios generally attract keener pricing from major banks. In contrast, a high-leverage project with minimal presales typically incurs a higher cost of capital. The experience and financial strength of the builder are also pivotal, as a reputable builder reduces the risk of project delays. The clarity of the exit strategy significantly impacts the lender's assessment.

Structural differences in drawdowns

Unlike standard residential mortgages where funds are advanced as a lump sum, construction finance utilises a progress draw structure. Funds are released incrementally as specific build milestones are reached, such as completing the frame stage or lock-up. These milestones must usually be verified by a quantity surveyor to ensure the work has been completed to the required standard. Lenders often hold a retention amount until practical completion is achieved. The retention serves as a security buffer, ensuring the builder rectifies any defects before final payment is released. Interest is generally capitalised during the build period, meaning it is added to the loan balance rather than paid monthly, which assists with cash flow management.

Bank versus non-bank appetite

Major banks maintain conservative policies regarding presale requirements and leverage limits, often rejecting viable projects that fall outside their strict credit boxes. Non-bank lenders assess deals more holistically, focusing on project feasibility and the strength of the exit strategy. While banks offer lower rates for low-risk transactions, private lenders facilitate construction loans for developers needing higher leverage or faster settlements. Non-bank lenders often open options for developers that remain closed under mainstream bank policy by assessing the deal on its specific merits. The decision involves weighing the cheaper cost of bank debt against the flexibility of non-bank funding.

Comparing capital structures

Developers must decide between a senior construction facility and a layered development finance stack. A senior facility may suffice for projects with modest leverage and strong presales. However, developers minimising their equity contribution might require mezzanine finance or preferred equity behind the senior debt. This layered structure increases total leverage but also raises the overall cost of capital. Layering capital allows developers to maximise their return on equity, although it increases the complexity of the capital stack. Comparing these options requires analysing whether the additional project equity retained justifies the interest expense paid to the subordinate lenders.

  1. Calculate Feasibility. Determine the Total Development Cost (TDC) and Gross Realisable Value (GRV) to establish the viability of the project.
  2. Review Presales. Assess the current level of presale contracts or deposits, as this heavily influences the lender's willingness to provide leverage.
  3. Evaluate Builder. Confirm the builder's license, experience, and financial standing to satisfy lender risk requirements and mitigate the risk of construction delays.
  4. Define Exit Strategy. Plan the takeout strategy clearly, identifying whether repayment will come from settlement sales or refinancing into residual stock finance.
  5. Compare Structures. Weigh the cost of capital against the return on equity to decide between senior debt only or a layered capital structure, considering the impact on the overall project yield.

Key takeaway

Commercial construction loans are sized using total development cost and gross realisable value rather than a simple loan-to-value ratio. Non-bank lenders often consider higher leverage and lower presale requirements than major banks, though pricing typically reflects the increased risk and complexity. Funds are released in stages through progress draws verified by a quantity surveyor, and interest is commonly capitalised during the build period to maximise cash flow efficiency.

Common questions

How does loan to cost differ from loan to value? Loan to cost (LTC) measures the debt against the total expense to build the project, while loan to value (LVR) measures the debt against the final worth of the completed property. Lenders use both to ensure security covers the budget and the end value.

Why is interest capitalised during construction? Interest is capitalised to improve the borrower's cash flow during the build phase when the property generates no income. The interest is added to the loan balance and paid out upon completion or sale.

When should a developer choose a non-bank lender? A developer should choose a non-bank lender when the project requires higher leverage than banks allow, has low presales, needs a faster settlement, or involves a non-standard structure that does not fit bank policy.
